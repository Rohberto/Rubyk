There's a particular kind of question that African founders get asked in investor meetings that their counterparts in other markets rarely face.

It's not usually stated directly. But it's always present.

*Can you actually do this?*

Not "is the market real" or "is the product ready." But something more fundamental — a baseline question of credibility that many founders spend enormous energy answering, often without realising that's what they're doing.

The good news: this is a solvable problem. The less good news: it takes longer than most people want.

## What credibility actually is

Credibility is not credentials. An MBA or a stint at a McKinsey doesn't confer credibility — it signals it, imperfectly, to certain audiences.

Real credibility is something different. It's the accumulated evidence that you understand your domain deeply, that your judgment can be trusted, and that your word means something.

It's built through three things: *what you say*, *what you do*, and *what others say about you*.

Most founders focus almost entirely on the third — testimonials, references, press. But the first two are what make the third possible.

## The content mistake

When we talk to founders about thought leadership, the reaction is usually one of two things: either they think it means writing LinkedIn posts about productivity, or they think it means something they don't have time for.

Both reactions are understandable. Both miss the point.

Thought leadership is not about being loud. It's about being *right*, on record, before the outcome is known.

The most valuable piece of content Victory ever wrote was Nigeria's first structured report on the informal economy — not because it went viral, but because two years later, people were still citing it. It became a reference. And being a reference is worth more than a hundred viral posts.

## The compounding effect of specific knowledge

Here's the thing about credibility in a specialised domain: it compounds in a way that generalist credibility doesn't.

If you spend 18 months going deep on one specific area — the logistics of last-mile delivery in secondary Nigerian cities, say, or the financing structures available to off-grid solar companies — you accumulate knowledge that is genuinely rare. Most people don't have the patience or the access to get that specific.

That specificity becomes an asset. When you speak about it, people listen differently. When you write about it, people save it. When you're in a room, people want to know what you think.

This is what we mean when we say: build the thing no one else can claim.

## Three ways to start building now

**1. Write one thing that takes real effort**

Not a hot take. Not a list post. One properly researched, genuinely useful piece of writing on something you know better than most people.

It could be a market analysis. A case study from your own company. A framework you've developed from experience. The bar is: would a serious person in your field find this genuinely valuable?

**2. Say true things publicly before they're obvious**

The early call — made carefully, made publicly, and made before consensus — is the thing that builds reputational capital. When you're right about something before most people see it, and you said so on record, that is worth more than any press feature.

**3. Be useful before you need something**

Make introductions. Share resources. Respond to the founder who asked for a thirty-minute call. The returns on generosity are slow and diffuse and then, one day, disproportionate.

## The honest version

None of this is fast. The founders we've worked with who have the most credibility didn't build it in a single quarter. They built it across years of doing real work, saying true things, and being consistent.

The temptation is to try to compress this — to manufacture credibility through a press push, a speaking slot, a reshuffled LinkedIn profile.

Those things have their place. But they work best when there's something real underneath them.

The long game isn't glamorous. But it's the only game that compounds.
